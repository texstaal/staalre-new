Build-to-Suit Warehouse in the Netherlands: Costs, Timeline and When It Beats Leasing (2026)

By Tex Staal · Founder & Advisor, Staal Real Estate
TL;DR
  • There are three ways to a new warehouse: a developer builds it and you lease it (build-to-suit), a developer builds it and you buy it on completion (turnkey), or you buy land and build it yourself.
  • Builders indicate roughly €400–800 per m² for a basic warehouse, excluding VAT and land, plus 20–30% for installations, site works and contingencies. Automation, cooling and offices push it higher.
  • Plan on 18 to 24 months or more from first plan to opening. The site, permits and grid connection usually take longer than construction.
  • A new build beats leasing when no existing building fits your specification, location or power needs, or when you plan to stay for a long time.

Who is this guide for? Companies that need a large warehouse, typically 5,000 m² or more, with requirements the existing market does not meet: extra clear height for automation, heavy floor loads, cold storage, many dock doors, hazardous goods, or a location where nothing suitable stands. If an existing building could work, start with our guide to setting up a distribution centre; it is faster.

Most international companies choose the build-to-suit lease: they get a building made for their operation without tying up capital. Owning makes sense for sites that are strategic for decades, or so specialised that no landlord would fund them at a reasonable rent. The general trade-off is covered in lease or buy for a distribution operation.

What does it cost to build a warehouse in the Netherlands?

There is no single price, but these are useful reference points:

  • Construction: Dutch builders indicate roughly €400 to €800 per m² for a distribution building, depending on specification and location, excluding VAT. They add around 20–30% for installations, site works and contingencies.
  • Municipal reference figures: municipalities publish standard construction costs to calculate permit fees. Hulst’s 2026 table, for example, uses €778 per m² gross floor area for a distribution centre over 10,000 m², and €945 per m² below that size. These are standard figures, not quotes, but they show the order of magnitude.
  • Land: priced per m² by the municipality or a private owner, and very different by region. Municipalities publish their land prices every year. You always buy more land than floor area, because the yard, truck parking, car parking and water storage need space too.
  • Everything else: permit fees (a percentage of the construction cost, set by each municipality), architect and advisors, soil research, the grid connection, utilities, sustainability certification such as BREEAM, financing during construction, and your own fit-out: racking, automation, IT.

An illustration, not a quote: a 10,000 m² building at €600 per m² is €6.0 million for construction. Add 25% for installations, site works and contingencies and you reach about €7.5 million, before land, VAT, fees and fit-out. A developer offering a build-to-suit lease prices all of that, plus its return, into the rent.

A realistic timeline

  1. Brief and site search: size, specification, region, power, budget. Usually 2 to 4 months.
  2. Feasibility: zoning, soil, access, grid capacity and nitrogen. The grid request should go in now, because it is often the longest lead time.
  3. Design and permit: an environment permit (omgevingsvergunning) under the Environment and Planning Act. The regular procedure takes 8 weeks, extendable by 6; if the plan deviates from the zoning or needs wider review, the extended procedure takes 26 weeks, also extendable by 6. Objections and appeals can add months.
  4. Construction: for a large steel-frame warehouse, often around 9 to 12 months.
  5. Fit-out and testing: racking, automation and IT, partly overlapping with construction.

Together that is rarely under 18 months, and 24 months or more is common when permits or power are not straightforward. If you need space sooner, lease an existing building first; our guide to short-term warehouse space covers the options in between.

Rules that shape a new warehouse in 2026

  • Zoning and XXL policy: the municipal zoning plan must allow your use and size. Provinces such as Noord-Brabant and Limburg steer very large distribution centres to a limited number of designated locations.
  • Nitrogen: construction and operations cause nitrogen deposition, so a calculation with the AERIUS tool is standard. Near protected nature areas a separate nature permit can be needed, which takes time.
  • Grid power: in congested regions a new or heavier connection can take years. See grid congestion and your warehouse.
  • Energy and solar: new buildings must meet the Dutch energy performance requirements (BENG). The revised EU buildings directive also requires solar installations on new non-residential buildings over 250 m² from the end of 2026, where technically and economically suitable. Check with your architect how the Dutch rules apply to your project.
  • Fire safety and hazardous goods: sprinkler requirements depend on size and use, and storing hazardous goods brings the PGS 15 rules into the design.

Build-to-suit lease: what to negotiate

  • The specification: a detailed technical description attached to the lease, with a clear process and price list for changes.
  • How the rent is set: usually linked to the total project cost. Agree how cost overruns and savings work, and cap them.
  • Conditions: the lease should only become unconditional once the permits are final and the grid connection is secured.
  • Delivery date: a firm date, compensation for late delivery and a right to walk away after a long delay.
  • Term and options: a long term is normal for a new build, but negotiate renewal options, expansion land and a right to sublet.
  • Reinstatement: agree now what you must remove at the end, especially for custom features.

Keep the specification as generic as your operation allows. The more specialised the building, the harder it is to let to the next tenant, and the higher the rent the developer asks to cover that risk. A standard shell with your own fit-out is often the cheapest route.

Build, buy or lease an existing building?

  • Lease an existing building when the market has something that fits and speed matters. See current warehouse rents.
  • Buy an existing building when you want to own and can adapt what exists. That costs 10.4% transfer tax; see the cost of buying a warehouse.
  • Build when nothing fits your specification, location or power needs, or you plan to stay for a long time.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to build a warehouse in the Netherlands?

Dutch builders indicate roughly €400 to €800 per m² for a basic distribution building, excluding VAT and land, plus around 20–30% for installations, site works and contingencies. Automation, cooling, heavy floors and offices push it higher. Land, permit fees, the grid connection and fit-out come on top.

How long does it take to build a warehouse in the Netherlands?

From first plan to opening, plan on 18 to 24 months or more. Construction of a large steel-frame warehouse often takes around 9 to 12 months, but finding a site, the permit and the grid connection usually take longer.

What is a build-to-suit lease?

A developer builds a warehouse to your specification and you sign a lease for it before construction starts, usually for a long term. You get a building made for your operation without investing the capital yourself.

Do you pay transfer tax or VAT on a new warehouse?

Usually VAT. A new building, up to two years after first use, and building land are normally subject to 21% VAT instead of the 10.4% transfer tax. A business with VAT-taxed activities can usually reclaim that VAT. Ask a tax advisor for your situation.

Do you need a permit to build a warehouse in the Netherlands?

Yes, an environment permit (omgevingsvergunning). The regular procedure takes 8 weeks, extendable by 6. If the plan deviates from the zoning plan, the extended procedure takes 26 weeks, also extendable by 6. Near protected nature areas a separate nature permit for nitrogen can be needed.
