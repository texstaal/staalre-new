Sale and Leaseback of a Warehouse in the Netherlands: How It Works (2026)

By Tex Staal · Founder & Advisor, Staal Real Estate
TL;DR
  • You sell your warehouse to an investor and sign a lease to stay. You free up capital and keep operating from the same building.
  • The price follows the rent and the lease term: an investor divides the rent by the yield they want. A longer lease or a higher rent raises the price, but you pay that rent for years.
  • The buyer normally pays 10.4% transfer tax on an existing building, and that comes off what they can offer you. Check VAT and tax on the gain with your tax advisor.
  • Agree the lease before you sell: rent, indexation, term, maintenance, expansion and any right to buy the building back.

What is a sale and leaseback? You sell the warehouse you own and use to an investor, and on the same day you sign a lease to stay in it as the tenant. In Dutch this is called verkopen en terughuren. Your operation does not move, but the money tied up in the building becomes available for the business.

When does a sale and leaseback make sense?

  • You need capital for the business: growth, automation, an acquisition or paying off debt.
  • Property is not your core business: you would rather put the money into operations that earn more than the building does.
  • Ownership and the business are separating: for example when an owner retires or the company is sold without the building.

It makes less sense if you expect to move within a few years, if the building is so specialised that few investors want it, or if the business cannot comfortably carry the rent for the whole term. Then a normal sale, or keeping the building, may be better. Our guide to selling a warehouse covers the other routes.

How the price is set

An investor buys an income stream, so the price starts with the rent. Roughly: price = annual rent ÷ the yield the investor wants, minus the investor’s own purchase costs. In the Netherlands the buyer of an existing commercial building pays 10.4% transfer tax, and that budget comes out of what they can pay you.

An example, for illustration only: a 5,000 m² warehouse let back at €80 per m² gives €400,000 rent a year. At a 6.0% yield an investor can spend about €6.7 million in total. After about 11% for transfer tax, notary and advice, that is roughly €6.0 million for you. Every half a percentage point of yield moves the price by about half a million euros.

The market sets that yield. According to Savills, prime net initial yields for Dutch logistics rose to 4.90% in Q3 2026, and logistics investment fell 32.1% on a year earlier to €371 million that quarter. Investors are more selective and pay for secure income: a long lease to a solid tenant in a modern building. Older buildings and regional locations sell at higher yields, which means lower prices.

Rent and lease term: the trade-off

What you agreeEffect on the priceEffect on you
Higher rentHigherHigher costs every year; hard to leave if the rent is above market
Longer leaseHigherLess flexibility; you commit for longer
Break optionsLowerMore flexibility to leave or shrink
Landlord does the major maintenanceLowerFewer surprises in your costs
Right to buy backLowerThe option to own the building again

A rent above market level gives you a higher price today, but you pay it back over the lease, and the building becomes harder to re-let or sell later. Most owners do best with a market rent and a lease term that matches how long they really expect to stay. Leases of ten to fifteen years are common in sale and leaseback deals with logistics investors.

Tax and costs to check

  • Transfer tax: 10.4% on an existing commercial building, normally paid by the buyer. See the cost of buying a warehouse.
  • VAT: a building sold within two years of first use is sold with VAT instead. If you deducted VAT on the building, selling without VAT within the ten-year revision period can mean paying part of that VAT back.
  • VAT on the lease: a lease with VAT is only possible if you use the building at least 90% for VAT-taxed activities. That matters to the investor and to the price.
  • Tax on the gain: the difference between the price and the book value is taxed as company profit.
  • Accounting: if your company reports under IFRS 16, the lease goes on your balance sheet.

The rules depend on your structure, so bring in a tax advisor before you agree a price.

The process, step by step

  1. Valuation and rent advice: what the building is worth to an investor, and at what rent.
  2. Lease terms first: rent, term, indexation, maintenance and options, agreed as heads of terms.
  3. Approach investors: a selected group, often off-market, so your staff and customers are not unsettled.
  4. Compare offers on price and on the lease terms each investor asks for, not on price alone.
  5. Buyer’s due diligence: title, zoning, building condition, environmental reports and your company accounts.
  6. Transfer at the notary, with the lease signed the same day.

What to agree before you sell

  • Rent and indexation: a market rent and a cap on annual increases. See negotiating a warehouse lease.
  • Term, renewals and breaks that match your plans.
  • Maintenance: who pays for the roof, the structure and the installations.
  • Expansion: rights to build on or take extra space on the site.
  • Buying back: a purchase option or a right of first refusal, if you may want the building again.
  • Assignment and subletting: important if you might sell the business later.
  • Reinstatement: what you must remove or restore when you eventually leave.

Frequently asked questions

What is a sale and leaseback?

You sell the building you own and use to an investor, and sign a lease to stay in it as the tenant. You free up capital without moving your operation.

How is the price of a sale and leaseback determined?

Mainly by the rent, the lease term and the yield investors want. Roughly, the price is the annual rent divided by the yield, minus the buyer’s purchase costs such as the 10.4% transfer tax. A longer lease to a solid tenant raises the price.

Who pays the transfer tax in a sale and leaseback?

Normally the buyer, at 10.4% on an existing commercial building in 2026. Because the investor includes it in their budget, it lowers what they can pay you. A building sold within two years of first use is sold with VAT instead.

How long is the lease in a sale and leaseback?

Leases of ten to fifteen years are common with logistics investors. A longer lease usually means a higher price, but less flexibility for you, so match it to how long you really expect to stay.

Can I buy the building back later?

Only if you agree it before the sale, as a purchase option or a right of first refusal in the contract. Investors may pay less for a building with a buy-back option, so weigh the price against the flexibility.
