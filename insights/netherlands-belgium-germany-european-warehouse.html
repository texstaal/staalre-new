Netherlands, Belgium or Germany: Where to Put Your European Warehouse (2026)

By Tex Staal · Founder & Advisor, Staal Real Estate
TL;DR
  • For modern space, the Netherlands is often cheaper than Germany: prime rents around Venlo and Tilburg are about €55–78 per m² a year, against about €96–106 in the Ruhr, Düsseldorf and Hamburg.
  • Import VAT deferral (Article 23 in the Netherlands, ET 14000 in Belgium) protects cash flow for goods from outside the EU; Germany mainly offers a payment delay against security.
  • Germany wins when nearly all customers are German; Belgium when flows run through Antwerp. The Netherlands wins for one European hub run in English, with Venlo and Limburg serving Germany at Dutch rents.
  • The Dutch weak spot is grid capacity: check the existing connection first. See how we help companies from abroad.

Should your European warehouse be in the Netherlands, Belgium or Germany? For most companies from outside Europe, the choice comes down to these three countries. They sit next to each other, share the big ports and motorways, and reach the same customers within a day. What separates them is rent, how imports are taxed, labour costs, grid capacity and where your customers actually are. This guide compares the three on facts, and says when each one wins.

Rent: Germany is not the cheap option any more

Many companies assume Germany is cheaper because it is bigger. In 2026 the opposite is often true for modern space. BNP Paribas Real Estate puts prime rents in Düsseldorf and Hamburg at €8.70–8.80 per m² a month, which is about €104–106 a year, and the Ruhr area at about €96. In the Netherlands, prime space around Venlo and in Limburg rents for about €55–72 a year, and around Tilburg–Waalwijk for about €60–78. Only Schiphol and Amsterdam are clearly more expensive than the German hubs. See warehouse rent prices by region for all ten Dutch regions.

That is why Venlo and Limburg are popular for companies whose main market is Germany: German customers within reach, at Dutch rents. Belgium sits in between, with Antwerp at about €68 a year.

Import VAT: the cash-flow difference

If your goods come from outside the EU, import VAT matters more than most people expect. On a container worth €200,000, 21% import VAT is €42,000 that has to be paid before the goods move, unless you can defer it.

  • Netherlands: with an Article 23 licence you declare the import VAT on your VAT return instead of paying it at the border. A Dutch BV can apply for it; a company not established in the Netherlands generally needs a fiscal representative.
  • Belgium: the ET 14000 licence works in a similar way, with a Belgian fiscal representative for companies from outside the EU.
  • Germany: import VAT can be paid later through a deferment account, but this usually needs security and is a payment delay rather than a shift to the VAT return.

This is one reason why so many non-EU companies import through Rotterdam or Antwerp, even when the goods end up in Germany. Check your own situation with a tax adviser; see also finding a warehouse from abroad.

Labour: available, but not cheap anywhere

All three are high-wage countries. Eurostat puts average hourly labour costs in the Netherlands at €47.9 in 2025, against an EU average of €34.9. Germany's minimum wage rises to €13.90 in 2026 and €14.60 in 2027; the Dutch minimum is €14.99 for workers aged 21 and over from July 2026. In practice, warehouse staff earn above the minimum in all three countries, and the real question is availability in your region. Busy logistics clusters such as Tilburg–Waalwijk and Venlo compete hard for people; compare regions in choosing a Dutch hub.

Power: the Dutch weak spot

The Netherlands has a serious grid congestion problem. Grid operators reported more than 15,000 requests waiting for a large offtake connection in mid-2026. For a plain storage warehouse that rarely matters. For electric trucks, charging, testing machines or cold storage it can decide the deal: the capacity already in the building is worth far more than a promise of an upgrade. Read grid congestion and your warehouse before you shortlist.

When each country wins

  • The Netherlands wins when you import from outside the EU and want import VAT deferral, need English as the working language, want a single European distribution centre that reaches most of Western Europe, or want to serve Germany from Venlo or Limburg at lower rents.
  • Belgium wins when your flows run through Antwerp, your customers are in Belgium and northern France, or you find a cheaper building in the Antwerp–Brussels corridor.
  • Germany wins when most of your customers are German and next-day delivery or on-site service in Germany is critical, or when you need to be close to German industrial customers in the south.

Many companies combine them: one main hub in the Netherlands or Belgium, and small service points in Germany once volumes grow.

Five questions that decide it for you

  1. Where do your goods arrive: sea freight into Rotterdam or Antwerp, air freight, or by road and rail from Asia?
  2. Where are your customers, by share of sales, and how fast do they expect delivery or service?
  3. Do you need import VAT deferral to protect cash flow?
  4. How much power do your operations need, now and in five years?
  5. Which working language will your local team use with headquarters?

Frequently asked questions

Is it cheaper to rent a warehouse in Germany or the Netherlands?

For modern logistics space, often the Netherlands. Prime rents in Düsseldorf and Hamburg are about €104–106 per m² a year in 2026, while Venlo and Limburg are about €55–72 and Tilburg–Waalwijk about €60–78. Only Schiphol and Amsterdam are clearly more expensive than the main German hubs.

Why do so many non-EU companies put their European warehouse in the Netherlands?

Because Rotterdam and Schiphol bring goods in fast, import VAT can be deferred with an Article 23 licence, English works as a business language, and most of Western Europe is within a day's drive. Invest in Holland reports that more than 1,000 American and Asian companies run their European distribution from the Netherlands.

Can I serve German customers from a Dutch warehouse?

Yes. Venlo and the rest of Limburg sit on the German border with motorway, rail and barge links into the Ruhr and beyond, so many companies serve Germany from there at Dutch rents.

Does Belgium have import VAT deferral like the Netherlands?

Yes. Belgium's ET 14000 licence also lets you declare import VAT on the VAT return instead of paying it at the border. Companies from outside the EU generally need a Belgian fiscal representative.

What is the biggest risk of a warehouse in the Netherlands?

Power. Many regions have long waiting lists for heavier grid connections, so if you need charging, testing or cooling, check the existing connection of each building before you shortlist it.
