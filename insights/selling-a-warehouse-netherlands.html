Selling a Warehouse in the Netherlands: Process, Costs and Tax (2026)

By Tex Staal · Founder & Advisor, Staal Real Estate
TL;DR
  • There are two buyer pools: owner-occupiers, who buy vacant buildings and compare prices per m², and investors, who buy let buildings and price them on the rent.
  • Investors value a let warehouse as roughly net rent ÷ yield. Prime Dutch logistics traded at a net initial yield of around 4.6% in 2025 (Knight Frank); most buildings trade above that.
  • Transfer tax, 10.4% on commercial property in 2026, is normally paid by the buyer. The seller pays the agent, the mortgage release and the documents buyers ask for.
  • A company that sells at a profit can usually defer the tax with a reinvestment reserve (herinvesteringsreserve) if it reinvests, in principle within three years.
  • Sale and leaseback releases capital while you keep operating from the same building.

How do you sell a warehouse in the Netherlands? Decide which buyer you are selling to, set a price that buyer can justify, prepare the documents they will check, and negotiate the conditions as carefully as the price. This guide covers each step for owners of warehouses and light-industrial buildings from 500 m², with the costs and tax points a seller should know in 2026.

Who buys warehouses in 2026

Owner-occupiers buy vacant buildings to run their own operation from, and are most active for units up to about 5,000 m². Investors buy let buildings for their income, from private investors buying a single unit to funds buying portfolios. Investment volumes are lower than a year ago: Cushman & Wakefield counts about €772 million of Dutch industrial and logistics investment in the first half of 2026, against €980 million a year earlier. Investors still buy, but they are selective: according to Knight Frank, vacancy is concentrated in older stock that no longer meets occupiers’ requirements, while modern, well-located buildings keep both their tenants and their value.

How your warehouse is priced

A vacant building: comparable sales and use value

An owner-occupier compares your building with others for sale, and with what leasing would cost. Location, size, condition, clear height, docks, the land (freehold or ground lease, erfpacht), zoning and energy performance drive the price per m². A building that needs work sells at a discount that reflects what the buyer will have to spend.

A let building: rent divided by yield

An investor values the rent. The price is roughly the net annual rent divided by the yield the investor requires. For a warehouse with a net rent of €400,000 a year:

One percentage point of yield is worth about a million euros here. What moves the yield: the remaining lease term, the strength of the tenant, the location, the quality and sustainability of the building, and how the rent compares with the market. For reference, Knight Frank puts the prime net initial yield for top-tier Dutch logistics at around 4.60% in 2025, with second-tier locations about half a percentage point higher. Smaller, older or shorter-let buildings trade at higher yields than that.

Sale and leaseback

If you own the building you operate from, you can sell it to an investor and sign a lease to stay. That releases capital for your business without moving. Investors pay most for a long lease, often ten years or more, at a market rent, with a financially strong tenant. There is a trade-off: a higher rent raises the price you receive, but also your costs for the years ahead. Agree the lease and the price together, because they are two sides of the same deal.

The sale, step by step

  1. Preparation. Title and cadastral data, zoning, permits, the energy label, floor plans, soil and asbestos reports where available and, for a let building, the leases and service-charge statements.
  2. Strategy and marketing. Owner-occupier or investor, a public campaign or a quiet sale to selected buyers.
  3. Viewings and questions. Buyers and their advisers inspect the building and ask for information.
  4. Offers and heads of terms. Price, conditions and timing.
  5. Purchase agreement. In practice the buyer usually provides a deposit or bank guarantee, often 10% of the price, and conditions such as financing, due diligence or permits give the buyer a way out until an agreed date.
  6. Transfer. The notary prepares the deed, the buyer pays, and the transfer is registered with the Kadaster. From agreed terms to transfer commonly takes two to four months.

If the building is let, the lease passes to the buyer: under Dutch law a sale does not end a lease (article 7:226 of the Civil Code). Buyers will want to see it, so have the lease, any side letters and the rent history ready.

Tax on the gain and the reinvestment reserve

If your company sells the building for more than its book value, the gain is taxable. If the company intends to reinvest, it can usually place the gain in a reinvestment reserve (herinvesteringsreserve) and defer the tax. The reinvestment must in principle follow within three years, and for a building the new asset generally has to fulfil the same economic function in the business. The intention to reinvest must be documented at the time, so get advice before you sign.

VAT if you reclaimed VAT on the building

If you reclaimed VAT when you bought or built the warehouse within the last ten years, a VAT-exempt sale can mean paying part of it back. Where the buyer uses the building at least 90% for VAT-taxed activities, seller and buyer can opt for a VAT-taxed sale instead.

How to get the best price

  • Sell to the right buyer. A building that suits both owner-occupiers and investors should be tested with both.
  • Extend leases before you sell. A longer remaining lease term lowers the yield investors require.
  • Fix the small things. A buyer’s surveyor prices every defect, usually at a premium.
  • Have the documents ready. Uncertainty costs money in the negotiation.
  • Negotiate the conditions, not only the price. A buyer without a financing condition and with a short deadline is worth more than a slightly higher bid with both.

Frequently asked questions

Who pays transfer tax when a commercial property is sold in the Netherlands?

The buyer, normally. Transfer tax on existing commercial property is 10.4% in 2026. A new building sold within two years of first use carries 21% VAT instead, and transfer tax then normally does not apply.

Do I pay tax on the profit when I sell my business premises?

Usually, yes: the gain over book value is taxed as profit. A company that intends to reinvest can usually defer the tax by forming a reinvestment reserve (herinvesteringsreserve) and reinvesting, in principle within three years. Ask your tax adviser before you sign.

Can I sell my warehouse with a tenant in it?

Yes. The lease passes to the buyer, because under Dutch law a sale does not end a lease. A let warehouse sells mainly to investors, who price it on the rent, the remaining lease term and the tenant.

How long does it take to sell a warehouse?

Finding a buyer and agreeing terms usually takes a few months, depending on the building and the pool of buyers. From agreed terms to transfer at the notary commonly takes another two to four months.

Is sale and leaseback a good idea?

It can be when you want to free up capital and stay in the building for the long term. You receive the sale price but pay rent for the years ahead, so it works best when the released capital has a clear use and the lease terms are fair.
