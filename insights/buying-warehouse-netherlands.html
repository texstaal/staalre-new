How to Buy a Warehouse in the Netherlands: A Guide for Foreign Companies

By Tex Staal · Founder & Advisor, Staal Real Estate
TL;DR
  • Foreign companies can buy Dutch commercial property without restrictions; decide early whether to buy through your own company or a Dutch BV.
  • The route: brief → search → offer → purchase agreement with conditions → due diligence → notarial transfer and Kadaster registration.
  • Budget roughly 10.4% transfer tax on top of the price (or 21% VAT on a new building), plus notary, due-diligence and financing costs.
  • Ready to start? See our buyer-side service to buy a warehouse in the Netherlands.

Buying a warehouse in the Netherlands is straightforward for a foreign company, but the Dutch process has specifics that decide both the price and the risk. The legal route is the same for Dutch and foreign buyers. What trips international buyers up is the order of things: signing before the checks are done, underestimating the costs, or finding out about a ground lease too late. Here is the process as it runs in practice.

1. Decide whether buying is the right call

Start with the case, not the building. Buying ties up capital and makes the location permanent. It earns its place when your fit-out is specialised, the site is scarce, or your horizon is long and stable. Model the purchase against leasing on total cost over ten years before you search; our article on lease or buy for a distribution operation walks through the trade-off.

2. Choose how you will own it

There are no restrictions on foreign ownership of Dutch commercial property. You can buy through your parent company, an existing Dutch entity, or a new Dutch BV. The choice affects tax, financing and an eventual sale, and it is much easier to get right before you sign than after, so settle it with a tax adviser first. If you are setting up in the Netherlands anyway, our setup guide covers KvK registration and banking.

3. Search on and off the market

Most large, modern warehouses are owned by institutional investors, who sell to other investors, usually with a tenant in place. Buildings that suit an owner-occupier, especially between 500 and 5,000 m², are more often held by private owners and business owners, and many are sold without ever being openly listed. A proper search covers listed stock, direct approaches to owners, and new-build or build-to-suit options where land is available. Our location guides show what each Dutch logistics hub offers.

4. Make an offer and agree heads of terms

A written offer is usually followed by heads of terms, covering price, timing and conditions, before the lawyers draft the purchase agreement. Until that agreement is signed, nothing is binding. Once it is, it is hard to get out of, so at this stage the conditions matter as much as the headline price.

5. Sign the purchase agreement, with the right conditions

The purchase agreement (koopovereenkomst) usually requires a deposit or bank guarantee of around 10% of the price, held by the notary. Protect yourself with conditions that let you walk away if:

  • Financing is not approved on acceptable terms.
  • Due diligence reveals material problems, such as soil contamination, asbestos or structural defects.
  • Zoning under the local omgevingsplan does not permit your use, or a permit you need is not granted.
  • Grid capacity for your operation is not available.

6. Run due diligence

The technical, environmental, legal and tax checks are what stop you from buying someone else’s problem. For a warehouse that means the roof and floor, fire safety, soil and asbestos, zoning and permits, title and any ground lease, and the capacity of the grid connection. Our warehouse due diligence checklist covers each item.

7. Complete at the notary

Dutch property transfers by notarial deed (akte van levering), signed at a civil-law notary (notaris) and registered in the Kadaster, the Dutch land registry. The notary checks the title, handles the funds and pays the transfer tax. From agreed terms to transfer commonly takes two to four months. What you pay on top of the price is set out in the cost of buying a warehouse in the Netherlands.

Financing the purchase

Dutch banks finance owner-occupied commercial property, but expect a larger equity contribution than on a home and close scrutiny of the operating company’s accounts. Line up financing early: a financing condition with a tight deadline is weak protection. We introduce clients to lenders and finance partners; see our partners.

Frequently asked questions

Can a foreign company buy property in the Netherlands?

Yes. There are no restrictions on foreign ownership of Dutch commercial property. The purchase process is the same as for a Dutch buyer: purchase agreement, due diligence, and transfer by notarial deed registered in the Kadaster.

Do I need a Dutch company to buy a warehouse in the Netherlands?

No. You can buy through your foreign company. Many buyers use a Dutch BV for tax, financing or operational reasons; decide the structure with a tax adviser before you sign the purchase agreement.

How long does it take to buy a warehouse in the Netherlands?

From agreed terms to transfer at the notary commonly takes two to four months, depending on due diligence, financing and permit conditions. The search itself depends on how scarce your type of building is.
